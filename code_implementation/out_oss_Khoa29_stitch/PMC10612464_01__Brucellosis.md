Based on the patient's clinical presentation of abdominal pain and jaundice, along with elevated transaminases and ALP levels, a preliminary diagnosis of probable vanishing bile duct syndrome (VBDS) was made. Probable VBDS is considered because of cholestatic imaging and elevated liver enzymes. The patient's symptoms of dizziness, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, joint pain, and fever up to 40°C, along with the physical examination findings of icteric sclera, right-upper-quadrant tenderness, a positive Murphy's sign, and percussion pain over the liver, support this diagnosis.

However, the patient's blood routine examination showed a decrease in WBC count, red blood cell count, and PLT count, which is unusual for VBDS. This atypical presentation raises the question of whether VBDS is the correct diagnosis. The patient underwent an extensive diagnostic workup, including blood culture (to exclude severe infection), all of which showed normal results. The antinuclear antibody spectrum (to exclude autoimmune disease) also showed normal results.

Additional investigations for infectious etiologies such as EBV, hepatitis A, B, C, and E, HIV, and syphilis all returned negative results. Despite these negative results, the patient's laboratory studies showed marked transaminase elevation, hyperbilirubinemia, elevated GGT and ALP, leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, which are not typical for VBDS. Further evaluation was pursued to determine the underlying cause of these cytopenias.

On hospital day 18, a bone marrow biopsy revealed the presence of haemophilus cells, meeting criteria for hemophagocytic syndrome (HPS) as the underlying cause of the probable VBDS. To determine the etiology of HPS, further testing for infectious indicators such as kala-azar and brucellosis was conducted. The results showed positive Brucella immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies, positive Brucella agglutination test (1:200 ++), and positive Brucella tiger red plate agglutination test (+), confirming Brucella as the infectious trigger for HPS.
