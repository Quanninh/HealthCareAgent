Considering the patient's presentation of acute decrease in left central vision, visual acuity was counting fingers in the left eye and 6/7.5 in the right eye. Examination revealed bilateral scotomas to confrontation and a localized creamy-white lesion of the left macula. Humphrey visual field testing identified a right arcuate defect and a left central defect, though reliability was reduced. Cilioretinal artery occlusion was considered due to acute vision loss, but fluorescein angiography demonstrated disk hyperfluorescence and possible infiltrative/compressive lesions of the left eye, making this diagnosis less likely.

Optic neuritis was entertained given visual field defects, yet computed tomography angiography of the head was normal and magnetic resonance imaging could not be performed; the lack of typical optic disc swelling lowered its likelihood. Radiological imaging was normal, further supporting the low likelihood of optic neuritis. Infiltrative/compressive etiologies, such as sarcoidosis and lymphoma, were evaluated; however, normal ACE level (40) and absence of mass on imaging made these unlikely.

Syphilis emerged as a potential diagnosis after serology showed a reactive rapid plasma reagin >1:512 and positive TPPA/EIA; the patient's history of MSM behavior supported exposure risk. Screening results returned positive syphilis serology (RPR, reactive >1:512; TPPA and enzyme immunoassay, reactive). HIV infection was excluded as a contributing factor, as the STI screen was negative for HIV. With negative STI screening, which included HIV, the focus remained on syphilis as the leading diagnosis.

Therapeutic response confirmed the diagnosis: visual acuity improved after extended IV benzylpenicillin therapy. Ophthalmology follow-up demonstrated improved visual acuity following completion of syphilis treatment. This improvement in visual acuity after treatment strongly supports the diagnosis of syphilis.
