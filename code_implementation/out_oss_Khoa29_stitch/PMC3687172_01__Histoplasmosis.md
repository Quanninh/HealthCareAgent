The patient presents with a two-week history of oral ulcers, fever, chest pain, and cough, together with new skin lesions over his face and forearm. The oral examination reveals ulcerated, necrotic lesions on the labial mucosa, lateral borders of the tongue, and hard and soft palate; the lesions are covered by a pseudomembrane and are extremely painful to palpation. Bilateral submandibular lymphadenopathy is present. Extra-orally, multiple nodular lesions are observed on the chin, right side of the face, and upper lip with an erythematous surface; hair loss is noted on the chin, upper lip, and eyebrows. Multiple pale plaques are present on the ventral forearm and dorsal thigh; they are warm, firm to palpation, and slightly raised above the surrounding skin. The patient reports malaise and fatigue. Vital signs are: temperature 38.2°C, pulse 82/min, respiratory rate 18/min, blood pressure 140/86 mm Hg. Laboratory studies show hemoglobin 9.4 g/dL, leukocyte count 7,800/µL (60% neutrophils, 36% lymphocytes, 4% eosinophils) and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 24 mm/h. Acid-fast bacillus examination of sputum is negative.

Considering the presence of oral and cutaneous lesions, disseminated fungal infection is a possibility. The cytoplasm of the histiocytes shows the presence of small round to oval basophilic bodies surrounded by a clear halo, which stained highly positive with Gomori's Methanimine Silver stain. This suggests the presence of intracellular yeast-like organisms that are silver-positive, consistent with a disseminated fungal infection.

However, pulmonary tuberculosis is also a consideration given the cough, fever, and chest pain. The patient's symptoms could be consistent with active TB, but the acid-fast bacillus (AFB) examination of sputum did not reveal any acid-fast bacilli (Zeihl Neelson stain). This makes active TB unlikely, although it cannot be entirely ruled out.

Another consideration is HIV-associated opportunistic infection, given the severity and disseminated nature of the disease. However, the patient was found to be HIV negative (comb and tridot tests), excluding HIV as a predisposing factor.

Taking all these factors into account, the most likely diagnosis is a disseminated fungal infection, specifically histoplasmosis. The presence of small round to oval basophilic bodies surrounded by a clear halo in the histiocytes, which stained highly positive with Gomori's Methanimine Silver stain, is consistent with Histoplasma capsulatum, the causative organism of histoplasmosis.
