The patient's presentation with sudden-onset, progressive bilateral lower-limb weakness, inability to stand or sit without support, urinary incontinence, and occasional fever and cough, along with the findings of areflexia of the knees, ankles and plantar responses, absent cremasteric reflexes, lax anal tone, and neurogenic bladder, suggests a serious underlying condition affecting the spinal cord. The MRI of the brain and entire spine demonstrated an intramedullary lesion extending from D11 to L1 with irregular margins in the conus medullaris and surrounding edema, which raises the possibility of neoplastic spinal cord tumors, such as astrocytoma or ependymoma. However, the differential diagnoses of astrocytoma, ependymoma, and granulomatous conditions were suspected, and the lesion's irregular margins suggested an inflammatory rather than classic neoplastic pattern, making neoplastic cells identified on cultures or serology less likely.

Further investigation revealed that granulomatous infectious or inflammatory conditions were entertained, and systematic exclusion included normal ACE ruling out sarcoidosis. Serum angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) level was noted to be within normal limits, which helped to rule out sarcoidosis. Immune-mediated demyelinating disorders, such as neuromyelitis optica and MOG-associated disease, were excluded by negative serum NMO and MOG antibodies. Brucellosis was considered due to endemic exposure but excluded by negative Brucella IgG/IgM serology. Additionally, ANA-positive connective-tissue disease was ruled out by a negative immunoblot ANA profile.

The patient's exposure to endemic schistosomiasis and the negative urine and stool examinations for Schistosoma eggs suggested a localized CNS process, but the possibility of systemic schistosomiasis could not be entirely ruled out. Tuberculosis was a key consideration, and Gene X-pert PCR and acid-fast bacilli stains on biopsy tissue were negative, and cultures showed no growth after 48 h, which helped to rule out tuberculosis.

Despite the extensive work-up, the definitive diagnosis was achieved by spinal cord biopsy revealing necrotising granulomatous inflammation containing Schistosoma eggs, confirming neuroschistosomiasis. This finding highlights the importance of considering local endemic conditions in the differential diagnosis, and the need for a thorough and systematic approach to diagnosis.
