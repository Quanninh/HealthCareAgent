However, these possibilities were excluded because the cell-based assay revealed a triple negative test result for serum AQP-4 Ab, MOG-Ab, and GFAP-Ab, and the MRI showed normal optic nerves without enhancement.

Next, we evaluated infectious etiologies such as tuberculosis, syphilis, herpesviruses, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis viruses, HIV, dengue, bunyavirus, and toxoplasmosis. Serum tests for these pathogens, including treponema pallidum, mycobacterium tuberculosis, herpes simplex virus, varicella-zoster virus, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, HIV, Dengue virus, novel bunyavirus, and toxoplasma gondii, were all negative.

We also considered systemic autoimmune vasculitis or connective-tissue disease, but this was excluded by negative ANA and ANCA. As well as antinuclear antibodies, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, were all negative.

At this point, primary antiphospholipid syndrome was entertained due to the elevated anticardiolipin and β2-glycoprotein-I IgM later, but the initial work-up did not yet measure these antibodies, and the clinical picture pointed toward an infectious cause.

The patient's lack of response to steroids and the concomitant febrile illness with an eschar made idiopathic steroid-refractory optic neuritis a less likely provisional diagnosis.

Given the eschar and regional lymphadenopathy, we considered the possibility of an infectious cause. After further investigation, scrub typhus (Orientia tsutsugamushi) was ultimately identified as the causative agent, confirming the diagnosis of optic neuritis secondary to scrub typhus.
