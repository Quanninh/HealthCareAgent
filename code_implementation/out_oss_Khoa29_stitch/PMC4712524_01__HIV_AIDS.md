The initial assessment favored a testicular tumor, but the heterogeneous echogenicity of the right testis on scrotal ultrasound and the pelvic computed tomography findings suggested a testicular tumor. However, serum tumor markers were within normal limits, and the histology revealed an inflammatory rather than neoplastic process, arguing against a testicular neoplasm. The lesion disclosed dense lymphoplasmacytic cell and histiocyte infiltration, and no well-formed granuloma or caseous necrosis was observed, further supporting this conclusion.

Considering the spindle-cell proliferation and plasma-cell infiltrate, IgG4-related disease was a potential diagnosis. However, the lack of IgG4-positive plasma cells excluded this etiology, and the absence of well-formed granulomas or caseous necrosis also argued against granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis or tuberculosis.

Special stains for acid-fast bacilli, periodic acid-Schiff, Grocott-Gomori methenamine-silver, and Gram organisms were all negative, making mycobacterial, fungal, and other bacterial infections unlikely. The absence of well-formed granulomas or caseous necrosis further supported this conclusion.

The presence of obliterative vasculitis and a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate are characteristic of syphilitic orchitis, directing suspicion toward a spirochetal infection. This led to the consideration of syphilis as a potential diagnosis.

Further investigation with T. pallidum immunohistochemistry and PCR confirmation revealed the presence of spirochetes, which cemented the diagnosis of syphilis.
