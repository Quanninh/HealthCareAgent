The patient's presentation of recurrent jaundice and severe pruritus over a 15-year period suggests a complex underlying etiology. Initially, we consider viral hepatitis A-E as a potential cause, but serum makers were negative for hepatitis A, B, C, D, and E viruses. The screening for toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus infections was negative, and deoxyribonucleic acid detection of Epstein-Barr virus and human cytomegalovirus were both negative, further excluding other viral infections.

Next, we evaluate the possibility of autoimmune liver disease, but autoimmune antibodies including anti-mitochondria antibody, anti-hepatorenal microsomal antibody, anti-hepatocyte solute antibody, and anti-soluble liver antigen antibody were negative. This rules out autoimmune liver disease as a potential cause.

We also consider Wilson disease, but serum levels of copper and ceruloplasmin were normal, indicating that Wilson disease is unlikely. Furthermore, ultrasonography revealed a normal liver echo texture and non-dilated biliary system, and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography confirmed the absence of dilatation of any intrahepatic or extrahepatic bile duct, making structural biliary obstruction an unlikely cause.

The patient's history of recurrent episodic cholestasis, which sometimes aggravated after administering penicillin and ibuprofen for treating common cold, suggests that drug-induced cholestasis may be a contributing factor. However, the fact that episodes occurred intermittently over years and resolved spontaneously without ongoing drug exposure makes this less likely.

Given the exclusion of other causes and the patient's clinical presentation, we consider an inherited canalicular transporter defect as a potential diagnosis. The recurrent episodic cholestasis, normal imaging, and biopsy showing zone 3 cholestasis all support this possibility. With the collected evidence, common liver diseases causing jaundice were excluded, and the cause of his obstructive cholestasis still remained unclear.

Based on the evidence, an inherited canalicular transporter defect, specifically BRIC-1, is favored as the most likely diagnosis.
