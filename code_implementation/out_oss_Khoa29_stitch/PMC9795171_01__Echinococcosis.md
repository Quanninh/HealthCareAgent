The imaging characteristics of the lesion, described as an encapsulated structure measuring 115 × 86 mm, with a thin capsule containing homogeneous liquid, are more typical of a hydatid cyst. Simple cysts are usually asymptomatic and lack eosinophilia, which is present in this case with an eosinophilia of 22.1%. This suggests that the lesion is likely a hydatid cyst.

However, another consideration is a hepatic abscess vs. hydatid cyst. The absence of fever, normal white-cell count, and lack of inflammatory signs make an abscess unlikely, and the presence of eosinophilia points toward a parasitic etiology. The patient denied fever, and eosinophilia (22.1%) was present, which further supports the diagnosis of a hydatid cyst.

The cardiac lesion, described as a 29 × 21 mm echo-lucent, intramural, drop-like structure bulging into the left ventricle, also needs to be considered. The lesion is non-enhancing, fluid-attenuated, and lacks vascular flow, which is more consistent with a cyst than a tumor. Tumors are often solid, may enhance with contrast, and can cause obstruction, but the cardiac lesion does not exhibit these characteristics.

Another consideration is a coronary artery aneurysm vs. cyst. The coronary MDCT excluded aneurysm and showed the cyst not in contact with coronary arteries, which further supports the diagnosis of a cyst.

Intracardiac thrombus vs. cyst is also a consideration. Thrombus would show echogenic material, possible attachment to endocardium, and often present with risk factors, but the mass is echo-lucent, unilocular, and lacks Doppler flow, which is more consistent with a cyst.

The patient's serology for other parasitic cysts, including Toxocara, Strongyloides, and Fasciola, was negative, but the epidemiologic exposure to dogs and eosinophilia favor Echinococcus despite negative ELISA. The cystic fluid contained larvae at different stages that had specific morphological characteristics of Echinococcus granulosus, which further confirms the diagnosis.
