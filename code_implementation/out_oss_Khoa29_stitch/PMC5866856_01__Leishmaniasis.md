The case of a 76-year-old woman with a 10-day history of right upper quadrant abdominal pain radiating to the back and progressive deep jaundice is presented. The patient had a history of primary myelofibrosis, arterial hypertension, and heterozygous β-thalassemia trait, but denied use of herbal agents, dietary supplements, illicit drugs, or alcohol. Physical examination showed jaundice and hepatosplenomegaly without hepatic encephalopathy.

Laboratory abnormalities included hemoglobin 10.8 g/dL, platelets 73 × 10^3/µL, INR 2.32, albumin 3.3 g/dL, total bilirubin 21.6 mg/dL (direct bilirubin 15.4 mg/dL), AST 717 IU/L, ALT 679 IU/L, γ-GT 56 IU/L, and IgG 1760 mg/dL (ULN 1500). Chest X-ray revealed left pleural effusion; ECG showed right bundle-branch block. Arterial blood gas demonstrated respiratory alkalosis (pH 7.52, pO2 68 mmHg, pCO2 28 mmHg, HCO3- 22.9 mEq/L).

Extensive infectious work-up, including blood cultures and serology for various pathogens, was negative. Autoimmune serology was positive for ANA (titer 1:80) and SMA (titer 1:320) with F-actin specificity confirmed by ELISA. Abdominal ultrasonography showed massive splenomegaly attributed to myelofibrosis and hepatomegaly with dilated splenic and portal veins, but no thrombosis in hepatic, portal, or splenic veins. CT pulmonary angiography excluded pulmonary embolism/thrombosis.

The differential diagnosis considered acute viral hepatitis, Budd-Chiari syndrome, ischemic hepatitis due to massive pulmonary embolism or thrombosis, and alcoholic hepatitis. However, acute viral hepatitis was excluded by comprehensive negative serologic and virologic testing. Acute Budd-Chiari syndrome and ischemic hepatitis as a consequence of massive pulmonary embolism or thrombosis were also considered. However, both diagnoses were excluded by imaging studies. Alcoholic hepatitis was excluded as there was no history of alcohol consumption.

The case of an idiosyncratic drug-induced liver injury (DILI) was seriously taken into account because the patient had a history of NSAID use and especially diclofenac administration for the last 7 days before admission. However, the presence of hypergammaglobulinemia, which is infrequent in DILI cases, and the positive liver autoimmune serology favored autoimmune hepatitis. The simplified score for the diagnosis of AIH without the histological data was 6 corresponding to the diagnosis of probable AIH.

Considering the patient's symptoms, laboratory results, and imaging studies, the most likely diagnosis is autoimmune hepatitis.
