Considering the case of a 21-year-old gravida 1 woman who underwent cesarean section at 33 weeks gestation for eclampsia, and was confirmed to have COVID-19 by clinical testing, vertical transmission of the virus to the neonate is a primary concern. The mother experienced fever for three days prior to surgery, accompanied by headache, dry cough, and shortness of breath, which made her a COVID-19 suspected case. Clinical tests confirmed the diagnosis of COVID-19, indicating that the neonate was exposed in utero and peripartum.

Given the prematurity of the neonate and low-grade oxygen desaturation, bacterial sepsis is considered. However, this is ruled out by negative blood cultures and normal CRP, as indicated by the fact that blood culture test results were negative; the patient's serum C-reactive protein was negative.

Despite the initial concern for COVID-19, later imaging showed perihilar infiltrates, suggesting typical neonatal viral/bacterial pneumonia (bronchopneumonia). This is supported by chest X-ray findings, which showed bilateral perihilar shadowing indicated of bronchopneumonia, and no sign of coronavirus was evident.

However, repeated real-time RT-PCR tests for SARS-CoV-2 from oropharynx swabs were negative on multiple days, including March 14 and March 17, and another oropharynx sample was also negative. Furthermore, lymphopenia, a common laboratory marker in COVID-19, was absent, further arguing against infection, as blood tests also showed no sign of lymphopenia.

Considering all the evidence, COVID-19 is the most likely diagnosis, despite the initial concerns for other conditions.
