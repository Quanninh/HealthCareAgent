The patient presents with a 2–3‑year history of mild pain and a pressure sensation over the right temporomandibular joint (TMJ) that worsened during mastication, jaw opening and closing, talking, and eating. The pain was described as dull, localized, and aggravated by speaking and taking food. She was unemployed and performed only domestic chores. On examination, there was no gross facial asymmetry, but opening the mouth produced a deviation of the jaw toward the left side, although mouth opening was acceptable. Palpation revealed a painless, soft mass in the right TMJ region with an “egg‑shell” crackling sensation and no audible joint sounds. TMJ movements were reduced on the right side. Intra‑orally, occlusion was satisfactory and gingiva were healthy. General health was normal and there was no cervical lymphadenopathy.

The presence of a soft mass in the right TMJ area with an "egg-shell cracking" sensation, along with a well-encapsulated fluid-filled cystic lesion on CT, suggests a cystic lesion of the TMJ, pointing toward a parasitic cyst rather than a solid tumor. The "well-encapsulated fluid-filled cystic lesion" on CT further supports this diagnosis.

A slight increase in eosinophil count is noted, which supports a parasitic etiology and helps exclude simple bone cysts or neoplastic processes. This finding is consistent with a parasitic infection, which is a key consideration in this case.

The normal liver ultrasound and chest radiograph argue against systemic dissemination of malignancy or pulmonary involvement typical of metastatic disease. This suggests that the condition is localized to the TMJ and is not part of a more widespread disease process.

The absence of systemic signs (fever, malaise) and lack of cervical lymphadenopathy make infectious osteomyelitis unlikely. This further supports the idea that the condition is a localized parasitic infection rather than an infectious process.

Definitive pathology later revealed daughter cysts with scolex, confirming hydatid disease (echinococcosis). This finding is consistent with the previous findings of a parasitic cyst and the presence of eosinophilia.

The combination of these findings leads to a diagnosis of echinococcosis, a parasitic infection caused by the Echinococcus tapeworm. This diagnosis is supported by the presence of a cystic lesion in the TMJ, eosinophilia, and the presence of daughter cysts with scolex in the definitive pathology.
