A 36-year-old man presented to the outpatient department with jaundice for 1.5 months and right-upper-quadrant pain for 1 week. He denied loss of appetite, weight loss, fever, or melena and reported no comorbidities. He was a temporary resident of Kashmir, the northernmost state of India, situated in the Himalayan foothills. Examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen with a hard mass palpable in the right upper quadrant. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen showed a large solid-cystic intrahepatic lesion measuring 125 × 118 × 123 mm involving hepatic segments IV, VII, and VIII. The lesion displayed peripheral subtle curvilinear and central nodular calcification. Arterial phase images demonstrated mild peripheral enhancement, with mild delayed phase enhancement as well. The lesion compressed the left portal vein and right main bile duct, causing right intrahepatic biliary radicle dilatation. Small cystic lesions were present in both liver lobes, the largest 23 × 18 mm, and a few enlarged peri-portal lymph nodes (largest 18 × 14 mm). Positron emission tomography-computed tomography revealed peripherally FDG-avid necrotic lesions with central calcification. Laboratory studies showed a mild eosinophil increase (9%) with normal hemoglobin and total leukocyte count. Liver function tests demonstrated mild conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, mild transaminase elevation, elevated alkaline phosphatase, and a reversed albumin:globulin ratio. Serum carcinoembryonic antigen, CA-19-9, and α-fetoprotein levels were within normal limits. A clinical possibility of a multifocal/metastatic centrally necrotizing malignant mass versus alveolar hydatid disease was considered, and a liver biopsy was planned.

The patient's residence in Kashmir, an endemic Himalayan region for Echinococcus multilocularis, supports a parasitic etiology. This is a crucial piece of information, as it points towards a specific type of parasitic infection that is common in this region. The patient's symptoms, including jaundice and right-upper-quadrant pain, are also consistent with a parasitic infection.

Furthermore, the patient's peripheral blood investigations showed a mild eosinophil increase (9%), which points towards a helminthic infection rather than a pure malignancy. This is an important finding, as it suggests that the patient's condition is likely caused by a parasitic infection, rather than a malignant process.

In addition, the patient's serum tumor markers, including CEA, CA-19-9, and AFP, were within normal limits. This makes primary hepatic malignancy less likely, as these markers are often elevated in cases of liver cancer.

The liver biopsy results showed a pattern classic for alveolar echinococcosis. The biopsy demonstrated PAS-positive refractile membranes, palisading histiocytes, and necrosis with no tumor. This morphology was classical to alveolar echinococcosis, and it provides strong evidence for this diagnosis.

Finally, the possibility of a multifocal/metastatic malignant mass was considered, but it was excluded by the absence of neoplastic cells on biopsy and lack of supporting clinical/laboratory evidence. This further supports the diagnosis of alveolar echinococcosis, as it rules out other potential causes of the patient's symptoms.

In conclusion, the combination of the patient's residence in Kashmir, the presence of a large solid-cystic intrahepatic lesion with peripheral subtle curvilinear and central nodular calcification, the mild eosinophil increase, the normal tumor markers, the biopsy results showing PAS-positive refractile membranes and palisading histiocytes, and the absence of neoplastic cells on biopsy all support the diagnosis of echinococcosis.
