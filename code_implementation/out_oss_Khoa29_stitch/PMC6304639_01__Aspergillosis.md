Given the concern for endogenous endophthalmitis in an immunocompromised patient with acute hypopyon, dense vitreous haze, and a retinal lesion, endogenous infectious endophthalmitis is a primary consideration. This is particularly concerning due to the patient's B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia in partial remission undergoing inpatient chemotherapy, which places him at high risk for opportunistic infections. 

However, bacterial endophthalmitis is considered but deemed less likely after cerebrospinal fluid and vitreous cultures showed no growth and systemic antibiotics had been administered. This suggests that the infection may not be of bacterial origin, but rather another type of pathogen.

Fungal endophthalmitis, particularly mold, remains possible despite negative cultures, given the patient's profound neutropenia and the presence of a pink retinal elevation suggestive of a fungal focus. Intraoperatively, a pink nonpigmented elevation was noted on the mid-peripheral superonasal retina, which is consistent with a fungal lesion.

In addition to the ocular findings, the patient's central nervous system lesions raise the differential of bacterial abscess, metastatic disease, or fungal infection. The rim-enhancing, diffusion-restricted lesions on MRI are compatible with fungal emboli, supporting a systemic fungal process. MRI brain illustrated rim-enhancing lesions with diffusion restriction in the right peritrigonal region and left corona radiata; orbital sections demonstrated circumferential smooth enhancement of the left globe and optic nerve sheath.

Flow cytometry to rule out intraocular malignancy demonstrated an immunophenotypically normal cell population and cytology of the vitreous did not show malignant cells, making uveitis masquerade syndrome (intraocular malignancy) less likely.

The combination of these findings, including the negative cultures, the presence of a fungal-like lesion, and the systemic fungal process suggested by the CNS lesions, points towards a diagnosis of fungal endophthalmitis. The ultimate diagnosis of Aspergillus infection was confirmed when retinochoroidal biopsy showed septate filamentous fungi with acute-angle branching characteristic of Aspergillus fumigatus.
