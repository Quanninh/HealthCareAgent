Pulmonary tuberculosis was considered the reason at the local hospital and therefore anti-tuberculosis was given. However, the cultures and clinical course did not support active TB, suggesting that tuberculosis was not the underlying cause of the patient's symptoms.

A combined infection of aspergillosis and Staphylococcus aureus was confirmed by positive culture of both sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). This explained the early cavitary disease, but not the later diffuse ground-glass changes, indicating that there may be another underlying process.

Based on elevated Aspergillus-specific IgE/IgG, high total IgE, eosinophilia, and bronchiectasis, a diagnosis of allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) was made. However, symptoms persisted despite antifungal and steroid therapy, suggesting another process was at play.

The next-generation sequence (NGS) of BALF indicated the presence of Human Herpes Virus Type 5 (also named CMV, sequence reads 1843). This was consistent with the diffuse ground-glass opacities seen on chest CT images, suggesting a possible CMV infection.

With classic diffuse ground-glass lesions appearing in the chest CT images and high sequence reads of CMV and P. jirovecii in BALF detected by mNGS, we identified that the patient had CMV and P. jirovecii coinfection. This was further supported by the response to sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim and ganciclovir, which led to clinical improvement.

The sequence reads of EBV, which is ubiquitous in most of the population, were too few to be identified as pathogenic. This, combined with the negative results for other autoimmune serologies, helped to exclude EBV as a cause of the pulmonary findings.

Serum -beta-D-glucan test, galactomannan test, anti-nuclear antibody, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, vasculitis antibodies, antiphospholipid antibodies, antibodies to extractable nuclear antigens, and rheumatoid factor were negative. This helped to rule out vasculitic etiologies and invasive fungal disease other than aspergillosis.

Considering all of the above, the most likely diagnosis is a coinfection with cytomegalovirus and pneumocystis, which was supported by the presence of both pathogens in the BALF, the characteristic ground-glass opacities on chest CT, and the response to treatment.
