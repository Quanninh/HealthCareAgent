Upon presentation, a 65-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus was seen with painless, progressive dim vision in both eyes over four months, the right eye more severely affected. The initial consideration was orbital meningioma due to the enhancing lesion extending through the optic foramen and onto the planum sphenoidale, mimicking typical meningioma imaging features. A working diagnosis of orbital meningioma was made.

However, intra-operative frozen section suggested a granulomatous process, prompting tubercular stains and cultures. This led to the consideration of granulomatous disease, including tuberculosis, as a possible etiology.

Further examination of the specimen revealed characteristic fungal elements, indicating a mycotic etiology rather than neoplastic or purely granulomatous disease. Histopathological examination showed multi-septate hyphae suggestive of fungus.

At this point, the primary diagnosis shifted to a fungal infection, specifically aspergillosis, given the presence of multi-septate hyphae. However, it was later noted that the patient also tested positive for tuberculosis, but this finding occurred after the primary fungal diagnosis had already been established and therefore served as a secondary co-infection rather than the primary cause of the orbital lesion.

The final diagnosis, therefore, is aspergillosis, supported by the presence of multi-septate hyphae and the absence of any other underlying conditions that could have caused the granulomatous reaction.
