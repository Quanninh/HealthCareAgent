The patient presents with an asymptomatic lesion on the dorsum of his right fifth finger that has been present for five years. The lesion began as small papules and later developed scaling. He denied any other skin or mucosal lesions, lymphadenopathy, sensory changes, pruritus, or systemic symptoms. Prior to this visit, he had received multiple courses of topical corticosteroids at various clinics without improvement. He had no known comorbidities, no exposure to chemicals or corrosive agents, and no chronic drug use. His work involved primarily typing. During the interview, after probing for possible exposures, he disclosed that he maintained a home aquarium as a leisure activity.

Physical examination revealed a hardened, rough-brownish erythematous plaque with a reddish-honey-colored scab on the dorsal aspect of the right fifth finger; the nail was spared. No palpable lymph nodes were appreciated in the upper limb or right axillary region.

The initial differential diagnosis included Mycobacterium marinum infection, contact dermatitis, and chromomycosis, with additional considerations of cutaneous tuberculosis, sporotrichosis, chromoblastomycosis, leishmaniasis, verruca vulgaris, sarcoidosis, foreign-body granuloma, tuberculoid leprosy, cat-scratch disease, psoriasis, and hypertrophic lichen planus.

Contact dermatitis is unlikely because the lesion persisted despite repeated topical corticosteroid therapy and the patient reported no itching or chemical exposures. He had received repeated prescriptions for topical corticosteroids with no evidence of improvement. He denied itching, as well as associated systemic manifestations.

Chromomycosis (chromoblastomycosis) was considered but histology did not reveal the characteristic pigmented sclerotic bodies seen in that infection. Histopathological findings showed pseudocarcinomatous epithelial hyperplasia with chronic granulomatous inflammatory reaction.

Cutaneous tuberculosis was deemed less probable due to the absence of caseation, lack of acid-fast bacilli on special stains, and no systemic signs. The absence of acid-fast bacilli on HE and Fite-Faraco stain was noted, and caseation is absent, but there is fibrinoid necrosis.

Sporotrichosis was lower on the list because the lesion was solitary and not distributed along lymphatic channels. The lesion is hardened and rough-brownish, and no palpable lymph nodes were found in the upper limb or right axillary region.

Leishmaniasis was unlikely given the patient's lack of travel to endemic regions and the histopathology lacking intracellular amastigotes.

Verruca vulgaris was excluded because the lesion was not hyperkeratotic or papillomatous and had persisted for years without the typical wart morphology. The lesion is described as a hardened, rough-brownish erythematous plaque with a reddish-honey-colored scab.

Sarcoidosis was considered but histology showed granulomas with fibrinoid necrosis rather than the non-necrotizing granulomas typical of sarcoidosis. Granulomatous inflammatory reaction of tuberculoid pattern, with a focus of fibrinoid necrosis, was observed.

Foreign-body granuloma was unlikely as there was no history of penetrating injury or retained material. He denied exposure to chemicals or corrosive agents.

Tuberculoid leprosy was ruled out because the patient reported no loss of sensation, paresthesia, or anesthesia in the lesion. He denied reduced sensitivity, paresthesia, or anesthesia in the lesion.

Cat-scratch disease was improbable due to lack of cat exposure and the chronic indolent course without lymphadenopathy. No palpable lymph nodes were found in the upper limb and/or right axillary region.

Psoriasis and hypertrophic lichen planus were unlikely because the lesion lacked the classic scaling, silvery plaque, or violaceous papules associated with those conditions. He denied itching, and the lesion description is not typical for psoriasis.

Mycobacterium marinum infection remained the most plausible diagnosis given the patient's aquarium exposure, the chronic granulomatous histology, and the absence of AFB on stains, which is characteristic for this organism. M. marinum is an environmental opportunistic mycobacteria, and the infection produces granulomatous lesions on the human skin. Granulomatous inflammation is most frequently found in M. marinum infections than other non-tuberculous mycobacteria.
