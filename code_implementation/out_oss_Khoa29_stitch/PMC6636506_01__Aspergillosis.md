The patient's presentation with low-grade fever and sudden, severe right upper-quadrant abdominal pain accompanied by nausea and vomiting, along with the emergent computed tomography findings of an intraluminal polypoid filling defect arising from the isthmus of the aorta, raises the concern for a cardiac embolic source. However, the transthoracic echocardiogram showed no findings to suggest valvular or cardiac source of embolus, and the agitated saline bubble contrast study was performed with no evidence of intracardiac shunting. This leads us to consider other potential causes of the aortic lesion.

Infectious etiologies, such as septic emboli or mycotic aneurysm, are also considered due to the patient's fever. However, the infectious work-up was negative and included testing for bacterial infection, fungal infection, HIV, Bartonella henselae antibody, Coxiella burnetii antibodies, Leptospira antibodies, syphilis, aspergillus galactomannan antigen, and histoplasma antigen. This suggests that an infectious cause is less likely.

A hypercoagulable disorder is also considered, given the presence of an aortic lesion. However, the hypercoagulability work-up was also unremarkable and included DRVVT screening for lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies, homocysteine levels, protein C and S activity, antithrombin mutations, and factor V Leiden and prothrombin genotyping. This makes a hypercoagulable disorder an unlikely explanation for the patient's condition.

Autoimmune vasculitis or connective-tissue disease is another potential cause of the aortic lesion. However, the autoimmune work-up was also unyielding and included testing for antinuclear antibody, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody, antidouble-stranded DNA antibody, rheumatoid factor, cyclic citrullinated peptide, Sjogren antibody, myeloperoxidase antibodies, proteinase 3 autoantibodies, complement levels, Smith antibody, and RNP antibody. This suggests that an autoimmune cause is also unlikely.

The presence of a large echogenic mobile mass attached to the wall of the descending thoracic aorta raises the concern for a primary aortic tumor, such as a sarcoma. However, the absence of tissue diagnosis and resolution with anticoagulation supported a thrombotic rather than neoplastic process.

Given the absence of an acute limb-threatening ischemia, the largely negative work-up, and the lack of a straightforward method for removing the aortic lesion, the team opted for medical management. The patient was transitioned from inpatient heparin to oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily and aspirin 81 mg daily, with plans for close outpatient follow-up.

The final diagnosis is an aortic thrombus, which is supported by the resolution with anticoagulation and the absence of other potential causes of the aortic lesion.
