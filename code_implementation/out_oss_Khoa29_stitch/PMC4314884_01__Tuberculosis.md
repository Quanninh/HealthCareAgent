However, this is excluded because "Touch smear and sputum were negative for tuberculous bacilli," "Mantoux test was negative," and "There was very little clinical response to the above treatment." This lack of response to ATT and negative diagnostic tests makes TB an unlikely diagnosis.

Another consideration is immunodeficiency, particularly HIV, given the patient's non-healing ulcers. However, this is excluded as the patient is "negative for HIV I and II." This rules out HIV as a contributing factor to the patient's condition.

A common bacterial infection, such as Staphylococcus aureus, is also considered given the pus culture results. However, this is excluded since "There was very little clinical response to the above treatment" with antibiotics, and the ulcers persisted. This suggests that the infection is not responding to standard antibiotic therapy.

At this point, nontuberculous mycobacterial infection (NTI) remains a plausible differential diagnosis. Definitive support for this comes when "culture was repeated, which this time grew non-pigmented, smooth colonies on Lowenstein-Jensen medium on 7th day of inoculation at 37°C," and "The isolate reduced nitrate, was urease positive and sensitive to amikacin." These results led to molecular identification as "M. chelonae-abscessus." This diagnosis is further confirmed by the successful targeted therapy with clarithromycin, which resulted in the healing of the ulcers within a short period of time.

Within 2 weeks of institution of clarithromycin, the axillary ulcer and sinus healed, and at the end of 40 days, the ulcer over the neck completely healed. This response to targeted therapy with clarithromycin further supports the diagnosis of M. chelonae-abscessus infection.
