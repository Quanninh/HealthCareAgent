The patient, a 63-year-old Chinese man with end-stage renal failure on continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD), presented with a two-day history of cloudy peritoneal effluent and mild intermittent colicky right-sided abdominal pain. The PD catheter exit-site was clean without any discharge, but the peritoneal effluent white-cell count was elevated at 1.6 × 10⁹/L with 63% neutrophils. Gram stain showed numerous polymorphonuclear leukocytes (2+) but no organisms, indicating a fastidious or atypical pathogen. The culture grew Weeksella virosa, a rare Gram-negative bacillus, which was sensitive to amikacin.

The patient was treated with intraperitoneal (IP) vancomycin and amikacin, and the effluent white-cell count normalized within four days. However, ten days after completing amikacin, he returned with cloudy effluent and abdominal pain, with an elevated effluent white-cell count of 6.9 × 10⁹/L with 74% neutrophils. Gram stain again showed many polymorphonuclear leukocytes (3+) without organisms. This recurrence with a different organism, Brevundimonas species, met the criteria for recurrent peritonitis with a new organism, prompting evaluation for a persistent source.

Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis did not show any intra-abdominal abscess or collection, ruling out a suppurative intra-abdominal focus. Cultures for fungi and acid-fast bacilli were negative, excluding fungal peritonitis and mycobacterial infection as causes of the relapsing episodes. The organism identification by API 20 NE (Weeksella) and VITEK 2 (Brevundimonas) was discordant, suggesting misidentification. 16S rRNA sequencing later clarified the pathogen as Dokdonella koreensis, confirming an atypical Gram-negative cause.

Despite appropriate aminoglycoside therapy and an intact catheter, the patient continued to experience recurrent episodes of peritonitis. The persistent infection pointed to the catheter as a potential nidus, leading to the recommendation for removal, which the patient refused. However, the patient was started on IP amikacin, and the effluent white-cell count normalized within five days. The sterile-technique instruction was reinforced, and he was discharged to complete the course.

The patient's presentation and laboratory findings are consistent with Dokdonella koreensis peritonitis, which is an atypical Gram-negative cause of peritonitis. The recurrent episodes of peritonitis, despite appropriate therapy, and the persistent infection point to the catheter as a potential nidus, but the patient's refusal to remove the catheter leaves the infection unresolved.
