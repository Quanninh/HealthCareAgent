Exposure to disturbed soil during golf course renovation was investigated as a possible source of the cluster, given that it is a classic risk factor for an endemic dimorphic fungal infection that incubates 1–3 weeks after exposure. The renovation work involved sod and tree removal, grading of existing soil (no soil was imported), and replacement of sod, which would have generated dust on the course during these activities. Workers reported that dust was generated on the course during these activities.

The approximate incubation periods of 6–12 days in the three symptomatic workers align with the typical window for this fungal infection. This is consistent with the incubation periods for an endemic dimorphic fungal infection, which would suggest that the workers were exposed to the pathogen during the renovation project.

Absence of bird or bat droppings and lack of known roosting sites argue against alternative fungal exposures that rely on avian or chiropteran reservoirs (e.g., certain histoplasma niches). No accumulated bird or bat droppings within facility buildings nor bird or bat roosting sites on the golf course were evident, which makes it less likely that the workers were exposed to a fungal pathogen associated with birds or bats.

Negative soil cultures do not exclude infection because the organism can be present in low concentrations and may not be recovered by standard culture methods. Soil samples were tested for the suspected fungal pathogen, but all were negative, which does not rule out the possibility of infection.

The pattern of a single occupational cluster with a shared exposure and limited secondary cases is characteristic of this infection rather than community-wide respiratory viruses or bacterial pneumonias, which would be expected to produce more widespread illness. No additional cases were identified among the 70 staff members who were invited to provide serum samples, and the workers who were symptomatic all reported onset of respiratory illness at the same time as the earlier cases.

Considering all these factors, the most likely diagnosis is an endemic dimorphic fungal infection, specifically histoplasmosis, which is a classic risk factor for exposure to aerosolized soil during renovation, has an incubation period of 1–3 weeks, and is not associated with bird or bat droppings.
