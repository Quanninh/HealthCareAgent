The patient, a 7-year-old girl from New Delhi, presented with symptoms of intermittent fever, weakness, and abdominal distension. Initial laboratory studies revealed pancytopenia, microcytic hypochromic red cells, fragmented erythrocytes, and a corrected reticulocyte count of 3.9%. The differential count showed N 30%, L 60%, M 9%, and E 1%. Serum ferritin was markedly elevated at 989 µg/L, triglycerides were high at 4 mmol/L, fibrinogen was low at 132 mg/dL, and lactate dehydrogenase was raised at 1,022 U/L. Liver function tests were deranged, but renal function was normal. Serologies for HIV, hepatitis B and C, EBV, CMV, measles, Widal test, K39 (visceral leishmaniasis), and rapid diagnostic tests for malarial antigen were negative. Blood, urine, sputum, and stool cultures were sterile. Chest radiograph and cerebrospinal fluid analysis were normal. Abdominal ultrasonography confirmed enlarged liver and spleen.

The peripheral smear revealed fragmented red blood cells and, on careful examination, schizonts of Plasmodium vivax, confirming a parasitic cause of HLH. This finding is crucial, as it identifies the underlying trigger for the patient's symptoms. Malaria infection is a known cause of Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), and the presence of schizonts of P. vivax supports this diagnosis.

Further investigation of the bone-marrow trephine biopsy revealed sheets and clusters of large histiocytes with abundant pale, bubbly cytoplasm. Many histiocytes demonstrated hemophagocytosis of erythroid precursors, leukocytes, and platelets; some contained brown pigment. Immunohistochemistry showed the histiocytes positive for CD68 and negative for CD1a, S100, CD3, CD20, epithelial membrane antigen, and pancytokeratin, ruling out Langerhans cell and malignant histiocytosis. The accompanying lymphocytes were CD3-positive. Special stains (PAS, Oil-red-O) were negative, excluding storage histiocytosis. Perl’s iron stain showed that most brown pigment did not stain for hemosiderin. The trephine biopsy was treated with alcoholic ammonium hydroxide, which bleached the brown pigment, confirming it to be malarial pigment (hemozoin).

The negative serologies and rapid tests excluded viral (EBV, CMV), bacterial (Widal), and other parasitic (leishmaniasis) triggers of HLH. Human immunodeficiency virus serology, hepatitis B surface antigen, antibodies against hepatitis C virus, the Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, measles, Widal test, K39 test for visceral leishmaniasis, and rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) for malarial antigen were negative. This rules out other potential causes of HLH, making malaria a more likely explanation for the patient's symptoms.
