Considering the patient's presentation with multiple left axillary lymph nodes and a history of furuncles, a provisional diagnosis of tuberculosis was made. The physical examination revealed discrete, firm, mobile lymph nodes, and the Montoux test was negative. Laboratory studies showed an elevated ESR, but other hematologic parameters and coagulation profile were normal. Chest radiograph and liver function tests were also unremarkable. Fine-needle aspiration of the largest node was performed, and the aspirate was blood-tinged and caseous. Smears stained with hematoxylin-eosin, Giemsa, and Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) were cellular, showing intact and degenerated neutrophils, histiocytes, eosinophils, loose epithelioid cell granulomas, giant cells, and a variable number of lymphocytes, with extensive caseous necrosis in the background. While screening the ZN-stained smears for acid-fast bacilli, classic hooklets of Echinococcus were observed – semi-translucent, retractile, sickle-shaped structures with an inner core of similar shape. A further search revealed large ovoid scolices. In addition, classic granular, beaded, acid-fast bacilli consistent with Mycobacterium tuberculosis were seen. This led to the consideration of tuberculous lymphadenitis due to caseous necrosis, epithelioid granulomas, and the presence of acid-fast bacilli; PCR and culture for M. tuberculosis were positive. However, the presence of characteristic hooklets and scolices of Echinococcus in the ZN stain raised the possibility of a co-existent echinococcal infection. Initially, the provisional diagnosis of tuberculosis alone was considered, but the discovery of parasitic hooklets prompted the addition of a co-existent echinococcal infection. The presence of both acid-fast bacilli consistent with M. tuberculosis and characteristic hooklets and scolices of Echinococcus in the same sample suggests that the patient has a dual infection, with tuberculosis and echinococcosis both contributing to the lymphadenopathy.
