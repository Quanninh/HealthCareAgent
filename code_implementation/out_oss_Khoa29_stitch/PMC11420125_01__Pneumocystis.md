Initially, bacterial pneumonia was considered due to the patient's clinical presentation of cough, fever, and infiltrates. However, this diagnosis was ruled out because sputum cultures did not isolate any respiratory pathogens, and there was a poor therapeutic effect despite broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Given the patient's immunosuppression, typical viral pneumonia, such as COVID-19, was also considered. However, no viral testing results are reported, and imaging lacked classic viral patterns. Furthermore, the lack of response to antivirals and the elevated serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan, also known as an increase in the G test, made viral etiology unlikely.

Considering the patient's severe immunosuppression, the combination of a dry cough with phlegm, bilateral patchy infiltrates on CT, and markedly elevated serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) became a leading consideration. This diagnosis was further supported by the lack of response to broad-spectrum antibiotics and the use of caspofungin, which had a poor anti-PJP effect.

Ultimately, the definitive confirmation of PJP was achieved when Pneumocystis jirovecii was detected in the next-generation sequencing of the patient's bronchoalveolar lavage fluid sample. This result validated the preceding reasoning and confirmed the diagnosis of pneumocystis.
