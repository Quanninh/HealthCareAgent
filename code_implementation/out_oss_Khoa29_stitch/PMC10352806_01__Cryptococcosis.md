The possibility of bacterial infection was first considered due to the patient's high WBC, CRP, procalcitonin, and fever. However, the patient was feverish and non-responsive to broad-spectrum antibiotics, which argued against it. The patient was empirically started on standard anti-tuberculosis therapy (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) on 15 December 2021, but remained unresponsive after six months, with worsening chest and back pain. The results of the acid-fast bacillus (AFB) test, smear, and cultures, as well as the lack of response to anti-tuberculosis treatment, argued against pulmonary and spinal tuberculosis. The patient's chronic cough, imaging showing osteolytic vertebral lesions, and endemic exposure made tuberculosis a primary concern, but the multiple negative microbiologic tests and lack of response to anti-TB therapy made it unlikely.

The patient's immunocompromised-like presentation led to the investigation of common opportunistic fungi, including Aspergillus, Cryptococcus, and Pneumocystis. The beta-1,3-D-glucan test, Aspergillus galactomannan test, Cryptococcus antigen test, and Pneumocystis japonicum DNA test were all negative, excluding these infections.

Malignancy, including primary lung tumor or metastatic disease, was considered due to the presence of pulmonary nodules. However, the absence of tumor cells on biopsy ruled it out. Tumors were not detected.

Nocardiosis was also contemplated as a cause of pulmonary and vertebral infection, but the lack of filamentous weakly acid-fast organisms on silver stain and negative mNGS bacterial reads made it unlikely. No other bacteria and parasite had been detected.

Autoimmune vasculitis or other inflammatory disorders were considered, but the negative ANA, ANCA, anti-CCP, anti-dsDNA, anti-keratin, and HLA-B27 made these diagnoses unlikely. We did not find any of the following antibodies in the patient: antinuclear antibodies, anti-double stranded DNA antibodies, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody, anti-keratin antibody, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, and HLA-B27.

After exhaustive exclusion of the above, the identification of intracellular yeast-like organisms on silver and PAS stains raised suspicion for a deep fungal pathogen. Hexamine silver (Figure 3A) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining (Figure 3B) were positive suspiciously, and eventually, we performed mNGS, which detected 1655 reads of Talaromyces spp. PCR amplification confirmed *T. marneffei* infection.
