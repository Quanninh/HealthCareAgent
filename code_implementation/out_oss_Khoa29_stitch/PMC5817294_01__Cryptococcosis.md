Considering the symptoms, an acute AIP crisis was suspected due to postoperative abdominal pain, vomiting, hyponatremia, and prior documented porphyria. However, this diagnosis was ruled out by the lack of porphyrin elevation at the time and persistent adrenal hormone abnormalities. Thus, an acute AIP crisis was considered but ultimately ruled out.

Given the postoperative stress, secondary (central) adrenal insufficiency was considered due to the markedly elevated ACTH (43 pM/L) indicating primary rather than secondary failure. ACTH was 43 pM/L and cortisol 163.3 nM/L, which suggests that the adrenal insufficiency is primary rather than secondary.

The patient's adrenal enlargement was evaluated for infectious causes, including tuberculosis, aspergillosis, CMV, and other fungal or parasitic infections. A tuberculin test was negative and so were tests on candida and aspergillus antigens. CMV DNA was undetectable, and other mycosis infections, such as cryptococcosis, histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, and trypanosomiasis, were ruled out through appropriate serological tests.

Autoimmune adrenalitis was also considered as a common cause of primary adrenal insufficiency. However, anti-adrenal antibodies were undetectable, making this diagnosis less likely.

The patient's postoperative state and bilateral enlargement raised the possibility of adrenal hemorrhage or infarction. Abdominal CT showed bilateral adrenal enlargement, with a hyperdense central area, but no hemorrhagic rim or acute bleed was identified, and the lesion disappeared on follow-up, making hemorrhage unlikely.

Drug-induced adrenal suppression was also considered, but the patient was not receiving glucocorticoids before admission, and no drugs known to impair adrenal function were administered. The patient was treated with no drug interfering with adrenal function.

Takotsubo cardiomyopathy and sepsis from postoperative pneumonia were evaluated as explanations for hyponatremia and hemodynamic instability. A chest X-ray was taken, which showed focal pneumonia with bilateral pleural effusion. However, despite resolution of infection, hyponatremia persisted, pointing to an adrenal cause.

Ultimately, primary adrenal insufficiency was supported by several lines of evidence. Firstly, the high ACTH with low cortisol levels suggested adrenal insufficiency. Secondly, the lack of cortisol response to ACTH stimulation (ACTH test 250 µg iv showed no response of cortisol) further supported this diagnosis. Thirdly, the low aldosterone/PRA ratio and low DHEA-S levels also pointed towards adrenal insufficiency. Fourthly, bilateral adrenal enlargement without infectious or autoimmune markers was consistent with primary adrenal insufficiency. Finally, the rapid clinical improvement after glucocorticoid replacement with oral cortisone acetate, which led to rapid improvement of symptoms and normalization of sodium levels, confirmed the diagnosis of primary adrenal insufficiency.
