A 76-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with a 1-day history of fever (38.3°C) and a 3-week history of headache. She was given a single dose of ciprofloxacin for a possible urinary tract infection and discharged home. Upon initial consideration, a possible urinary tract infection was considered because of fever and initial empiric ciprofloxacin therapy. She received one dose of ciprofloxacin for a possible urinary tract infection and was discharged.

However, the initial Gram-stain interpretation as coryneform bacilli suggested a contaminant or non-specific gram-negative rod. A Gram stain of this patient's blood culture specimen showed tiny, gram-negative coccobacilli, reported as coryneform bacilli. This raised some concern about the accuracy of the initial identification.

Further review of the Gram-stain slide revealed small, gram-variable cocci, which suggested a fastidious organism such as Brucella, which can appear gram-variable and be misidentified as coryneform. The slide was referred to the Wadsworth Center, where small, gram-variable cocci were identified.

Despite the lack of classic risk factors for brucellosis, epidemiologic clues supported exposure risk for the disease. Upon interview, the patient denied any visits outside the United States since 1989, when she emigrated from Peru. She also denied consuming any unpasteurized products. This information, although not typical for brucellosis, raised suspicion for the disease.

To confirm the diagnosis, definitive confirmation by PCR detecting B. melitensis DNA was performed. Polymerase chain reaction tests performed on Gram stain material detected B. melitensis DNA. This result established brucellosis as the causative agent, ruling out other gram-negative coccobacilli.

In conclusion, the combination of the patient's symptoms, the initial Gram-stain results, the re-evaluation of the slide, the epidemiologic clues, and the definitive confirmation by PCR all point to a diagnosis of brucellosis.
