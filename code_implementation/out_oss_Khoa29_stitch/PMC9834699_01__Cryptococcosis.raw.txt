Metastatic recurrence of NSCLC was high on the differential because of prior widespread disease and new pulmonary lesion; however, pathology demonstrated fungal elements rather than malignant cells. Fungi showing positive Grocott staining confirmed pulmonary cryptococcosis. This led us to consider alternative explanations for the patient's symptoms and imaging findings.

EGFR T790M mutation was sought to guide next-line targeted therapy; liquid biopsy later proved negative, indicating that the lesion was not driven by this resistance mutation. This result ruled out one potential cause of the patient's pulmonary lesion, but did not provide a clear diagnosis.

Post-biopsy pulmonary hemorrhage was considered for the ground-glass opacity; CT excluded pneumothorax or other complications, and the opacity was judged to be due to hemoptysis. However, this finding did not fully explain the presence of the pulmonary lesion itself.

Pulmonary infection, particularly fungal, was entertained after the hemorrhagic finding; definitive diagnosis came from Grocott stain revealing fungal organisms. Fungi showing positive Grocott staining confirmed pulmonary cryptococcosis. This result provided a clear explanation for the patient's symptoms and imaging findings.

Cryptococcal meningitis was evaluated because of potential dissemination; CSF cryptococcal antigen, cytology, and cultures were negative, ruling out CNS involvement. Serum and cerebrospinal fluid cryptococcal glucuronoxylomannan antigens were negative. Cerebrospinal fluid cytology and culture excluded cryptococcal meningitis. This result further supported the diagnosis of pulmonary cryptococcosis, as it ruled out the possibility of CNS involvement.

Considering all of these findings, the most likely diagnosis is cryptococcosis. The patient's symptoms and imaging findings are consistent with this diagnosis, and the results of the Grocott stain and CSF studies provide strong evidence for this conclusion.
