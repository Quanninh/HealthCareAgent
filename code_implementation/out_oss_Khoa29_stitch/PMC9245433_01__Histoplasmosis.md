The patient presents with a one-month history of intermittent fever, fatigue, anorexia, and a 30-kg weight loss over five months. The combination of these symptoms, particularly the fever and hepatosplenomegaly, raises the suspicion of a fungal infection. The bone-marrow aspiration showed hemophagocytosis and yeast-like organisms, further supporting this suspicion. However, the marrow culture was negative, and the patient was empirically treated with voriconazole, which was later switched to itraconazole without clinical improvement.

Upon further examination, the patient's laboratory studies reveal pancytopenia, CRP 44.4 mg/L, procalcitonin 1.65 ng/mL, fibrinogen 1.0 g/L, ferritin 2,775 ng/mL, and an elevated (1→3)-β-d-glucan level. These findings are consistent with a disseminated fungal infection. The presence of intracellular yeasts with characteristic morphology on marrow histology, specifically oval or round organisms with amaranth nuclei and capsule-like unstained halos within phagocytes, is also suggestive of a fungal infection.

Lymphoma was considered as a possible diagnosis due to the PET/CT findings of high FDG uptake in bone marrow, suggesting lymphoma probably. However, the presence of intracellular yeasts and the rapid clinical response to antifungal therapy argue against primary malignancy.

Other dimorphic fungal infections, such as Blastomyces and Coccidioides, were considered in the differential diagnosis. However, the negative galactomannan level and the specific morphological features of the organisms favor Histoplasma. The isolate later grew on PDA at 25°C, consistent with Histoplasma capsulatum.

Tuberculosis was excluded by a negative T-cell spot test and the lack of acid-fast organisms on smear. The (1→3)-β-d-glucan elevation is more typical of fungal rather than mycobacterial infection.

Viral triggers of HLH, such as EBV, CMV, and HIV, were ruled out by negative serologies, making an infectious fungal trigger more plausible. The patient's prior health suggests that primary immunodeficiency is unlikely, and the low CD4 count is likely secondary to HLH and resolved after treatment, supporting an acquired, infection-driven process.

Considering all these findings, the most likely diagnosis is disseminated histoplasmosis, which is consistent with the combination of fever, hepatosplenomegaly, marked hyperferritinemia, cytopenias, elevated (1→3)-β-d-glucan, and intracellular yeasts with characteristic morphology on marrow histology.
