The patient's presentation of fever of unknown origin, past medical history of aortic valve replacement, and monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) prompts consideration of prosthetic valve endocarditis. However, the transthoracic echocardiogram did not show vegetation or valvular dysfunction, and the transesophageal echo was limited by a prosthesis shadow cone, making culture-negative prosthetic valve endocarditis less likely. The patient's broad infectious work-up was negative for common bacterial and viral pathogens, reducing the likelihood of alternative infections. Tests for Legionella pneumophila, Treponema pallidum, Coxsackie virus A and B, Parvovirus B19, Leishmania spp., Toxoplasma gondii, HIV, and Hepatitis B and C were negative.

The patient's serology revealed a past infection of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), but peripheral blood molecular testing indicated potential EBV reactivation as 3,359 DNA copies/mL were detected. However, this is unlikely to be the primary cause of the prolonged fever, given the low viral load and past infection status. Autoimmune disease is also entertained, given the elevated antinuclear antibody (ANA) and rheumatoid factor (RF) titers. However, high auto-antibody titers are also reported in infections, leading us to favor an infectious etiology. Because it has been described in the literature that high Abs titers toward ANAs have been frequently detected in nonautoimmune individuals with bacterial or viral infection, an infective cause was considered.

The patient's laboratory evaluation revealed anemia, moderate cytopenia, elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate, polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia, thrombocytopenia, and moderately increased C-reactive protein and procalcitonin. The positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) showed a picture compatible with periaortitis, an intensive uptake of the splenic medulla, compatible with a reactive-infectious condition and/or hematological disease. This raises the possibility of a hematologic disease related to the patient's MGUS, but imaging could not differentiate between reactive infection and hematologic pathology.

Further investigation revealed high titers of Immunoglobulin G and M, both against anti-phase I and II of Coxiella burnetii, which were detected (phase I IgM 3,200, phase II IgM 800, phase I IgG 3,200, and phase II IgG 3,200). This satisfies the modified Duke criteria for chronic Q fever.
