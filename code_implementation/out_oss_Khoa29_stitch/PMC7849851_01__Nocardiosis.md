Considering the patient's presentation of pain, redness, and photophobia in the left eye following a history of trauma, post-traumatic hyphema with secondary glaucoma was initially considered. This diagnosis was supported by the presence of hyphema and elevated intraocular pressure, and the child was started on topical antiglaucoma and cycloplegics. However, imaging studies, including ultrasound B-scan, showed no evidence of any intraocular foreign body, mass lesion, or calcification, which ruled out these potential causes.

Further investigation with CT-scan orbit revealed no evidence of an intraocular mass lesion or calcification, which also made an intraocular tumor or mass lesion less likely. Additionally, systemic examination and investigations were negative, suggesting that endogenous (systemic) infection was unlikely.

Despite the initial response to treatment, the eye condition worsened, and the patient developed proptosis, lid edema, and restricted movement. Repeat imaging studies, including B-scan and orbital CT, showed new findings suggestive of multiple vitreous abscesses, thickened sclera and choroid, and a ring-enhancing intraconal lesion consistent with an intraconal abscess. These findings were consistent with a diagnosis of panophthalmitis, an infection of the entire eye.

However, the lack of response to broad-spectrum systemic antibiotics suggested that an atypical organism may be responsible for the infection. As there was no improvement in the eye condition after 24 hours of systemic broad-spectrum antibiotics, evisceration was planned. Further microbiological and histopathological evaluation of the intraocular contents was performed, which ultimately revealed the presence of Nocardia. On Gram stains, mildly Gram-positive beaded filaments were observed, and chalky white colonies grew on the Chocolate agar 3 days after inoculation. Smear from culture confirmed the presence of Nocardia, leading to a diagnosis of nocardiosis.
