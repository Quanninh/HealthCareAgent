A 73-year-old man presents with a two-month history of general weakness, poor appetite, mild exertional dyspnea, and cough. Chest X-ray at another hospital had shown multiple cavitary lung lesions. He has a 2-year history of diabetes mellitus and hypertension, a 70-pack-year smoking history, and no recent travel, blood transfusions, IV drug use, or high-risk sexual behavior.

On admission, his blood pressure was 110/90 mm Hg, pulse 94/min, respiratory rate 22/min, temperature 36.2 °C. Physical examination revealed coarse crackles in both lower lung fields; there were no skin lesions, lymphadenopathy, or splenomegaly.

Laboratory studies: WBC 7,300/mm³ (neutrophils 86.4%, lymphocytes 7%), hemoglobin 11.5 g/dL, platelets 464 ×10³/µL, glucose 109 mg/dL, AST 19 IU/L, ALT 25 IU/L, LDH 448 IU/L, HbA1c 6.4%. Urinalysis was normal.

A sputum gram stain and bacterial culture revealed no organisms, making bacterial pneumonia less likely. However, the patient's symptoms and radiographic findings suggest a more complex etiology.

Tuberculosis is considered because of the cavitary lesions, but direct examinations and sputum culture were negative for acid-fast organisms, making this diagnosis less likely.

The patient's history and physical examination suggest a possible malignancy, and histologic findings of the endobronchial biopsy showed moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, explaining the nodular lesions but not the diffuse cavitary pattern.

However, the presence of granulomas with central necrosis in the transbronchial lung biopsy suggests an opportunistic infection, particularly in the context of a malignancy. HIV-associated immunosuppression is considered due to the patient's age and symptoms, but HIV antibodies were negative, and HIV antigen (P24 Ag) was not detectable, making this diagnosis unlikely.

The presence of granulomas with necrosis and the patient's symptoms suggest an opportunistic fungal infection, and percutaneous needle aspiration cytology revealed yeast-form fungi, consistent with cryptococci. Furthermore, serum cryptococcal antigen was positive (1:16 titer), confirming the diagnosis of cryptococcosis.
