A 62-year-old man in close contact with sheep presented with a one-month history of intermittent fever, fatigue, night sweats, and progressive low back pain. The patient's occupation and close contact with sheep raises the risk for zoonotic brucellosis, a zoonotic disease that can be transmitted from animals to humans. This is a critical consideration given the patient's exposure to sheep.

The patient's symptoms, including intermittent fever, night sweats, fatigue, and progressive low back pain, are systemic manifestations of brucellosis. These symptoms are consistent with the classic presentation of brucellosis, which often includes fever, fatigue, and musculoskeletal pain.

The patient's physical examination revealed percussion pain over the L3 and L4 vertebrae, suggesting a spinal infection. This finding is consistent with brucellar spondylitis, a form of brucellosis that affects the spine.

Laboratory data showed a white blood cell count of 5.6 × 10^9/L with 59.5% neutrophils and 27.8% lymphocytes, which is within normal limits. However, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was markedly elevated at 107 mm/h, and the C-reactive protein (CRP) was also elevated at 65 mg/L. These findings indicate an inflammatory or infectious process, which is consistent with brucellosis.

Computed tomography of the lumbar spine demonstrated apparent destruction at the anterior superior corner of the L4 vertebra, known as Pedro Pons' sign. This radiologic finding is a characteristic feature of brucellar spondylitis, further supporting the diagnosis of brucellosis.

Magnetic resonance imaging showed bone destruction of the L3 and L4 vertebrae with adjacent paravertebral abscesses, which is consistent with infectious spondylitis. This finding supports the diagnosis of brucellosis over non-infectious causes.

Finally, definitive confirmation was obtained by isolating Brucella ovis from the abscess and by a high-titer Brucella serum agglutination test. The isolation of Brucella ovis from the abscess and the positive serum agglutination test confirm the diagnosis of brucellosis.

In conclusion, the patient's close contact with sheep, systemic symptoms, spinal infection, elevated inflammatory markers, characteristic radiologic findings, and definitive laboratory confirmation all support the diagnosis of brucellosis.
