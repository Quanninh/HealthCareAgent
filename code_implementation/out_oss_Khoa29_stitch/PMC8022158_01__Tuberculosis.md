Tuberculous pericarditis was initially favored because of endemic exposure, a positive QuantiFERON-Gold test, and a lymphocyte-predominant pericardial effusion. However, AFB smears and PCR were negative, however QuantiFERON test returned positive. A presumptive diagnosis of pericardial tuberculosis was made, but the final Mycobacterial Tuberculosis culture from sputum and pericardial fluid was negative.

This led to consideration of alternative explanations for the patient's symptoms and lab results. Persistent and rising peripheral eosinophilia (from 9% to 12%) suggested a parasitic etiology rather than bacterial infection; eosinophilia is atypical for TB pericarditis. Lab investigations however were significant for an increase in peripheral eosinophilia (12%; absolute eosinophil count of 1.0 × 10^3/µL).

Further investigation revealed thoracoscopic visualization of white, pearly nodules on pleural and pericardial surfaces, which is characteristic of parasitic granulomatous disease and not typical for TB. This directed tissue sampling. Multiple white pearly nodules on the parietal pleura and to lesser extent on the pericardium and visceral pleura were found.

Histopathology of pleural biopsy showed granulomas engulfing an ova with thick cuticle that was polarizable, a finding pathognomonic for Paragonimus westermani infection, thereby confirming the diagnosis and excluding TB.
