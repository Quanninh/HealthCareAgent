Considering the patient's presentation of nonproductive cough without fever or dyspnea, and the absence of lymphadenopathy or chest auscultation abnormalities, bacterial pneumonia is considered but deemed unlikely because "sputum showed no predominant pathogen and no acid-fast organisms were observed on staining" and "two sets of blood cultures... did not yield any organisms." This led us to consider viral or atypical infection, but it was excluded by the normal immunoglobulin levels and negative serologies for common viral markers, as indicated by "the serum was negative for rheumatoid factor and antinuclear antibodies."

The patient's imaging studies, including chest CT, revealed bilateral diffuse infiltrates and ground-glass opacity, which, combined with histology showing "alveolar septal thickening due to chronic inflammation, as well as collagen-type fibrosis," led us to initially diagnose acute interstitial pneumonia. However, after administering high-dose steroids and cyclophosphamide, drug-induced lung injury was considered, but it was less likely because the clinical picture preceded immunosuppressive therapy and the elevation of the serum (1-3) beta-D glucan level suggested a fungal component.

Given the beta-D glucan rise, other fungal infection was considered, but "routine cultures of bronchial washings were negative" and later GMS staining identified only *Pneumocystis jirovecii*, ruling out other fungi. This led us to consider Pneumocystis pneumonia (PCP), and post-mortem lung pathology showed "abundant numbers of cystic forms of Pneumocystis jirovecii." Furthermore, retrospectively, GMS staining of the BALF and TBLB samples revealed a small amount of the cystic form of Pneumocystis jirovecii, confirming that PCP was present on admission.
