She was started on intravenous (IV) antibiotics for community-acquired pneumonia, given the symptoms of cough, fever, and infiltrates. However, despite therapy, her oxygen requirement rose daily, progressing from 4 L nasal cannula to 60% venturi mask, and arterial blood gases persisted with type 1 respiratory failure. This lack of clinical improvement prompted further work-up, leading to consideration of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) due to the interstitial pattern and immunocompetent status not being excluded. Cotrimoxazole was given empirically for PCP, and oral glucocorticoids were also added for suspected PCP pneumonia. Yet, no response was observed, suggesting that PCP might not be the primary cause.

Further investigation revealed non-specific interstitial pneumonitis (NSIP) suggested by HRCT findings of ground-glass opacities with fibrotic changes. Autoimmune serologies were negative, making idiopathic NSIP plausible but not definitive. Tuberculosis was also investigated via acid-fast bacilli staining and NAAT on bronchial washings because of chronic symptoms and radiographic abnormalities, but results were negative, ruling it out. Similarly, nocardiosis was screened given the subacute pulmonary infiltrates, but cultures were negative, excluding this pathogen. Fungal infections, such as aspergillosis and histoplasmosis, were considered and fungal cultures performed, but negative results made these unlikely.

Careful examination revealed herpetic lesions with vesicles at the C3 dermatomal level, which led to consideration of disseminated varicella infection with varicella pneumonia. This diagnosis emerged as a possibility after the initial diagnoses of community-acquired pneumonia, PCP, and NSIP were ruled out. The initiation of acyclovir led to rapid clinical recovery, confirming the diagnosis of varicella pneumonia.
